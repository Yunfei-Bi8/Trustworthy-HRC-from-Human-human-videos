A robot arm learns to work with a person from videos of two people doing the task together, even in roles no robot ever demonstrated (zero-shot). A safety layer checks whether the robot's own gripper can really do what the humans did, and stops the robot when it cannot.

Two contributions

Tested on a real UR10e robot arm: 3 tasks, both roles of each task, 20 runs per role.

Contribution 1

Human-robot collaboration learned from human-human videos

The robot learns handovers and jar opening from ordinary videos of two people. It needs at most 5 robot demonstrations per task, and none at all for the zero-shot roles, which it learns purely from the human-human videos.

18 & 17/20

successful runs in the bowl handover and jar opening with 5 robot demos each; only 5 and 4 without the human videos

13 & 14/20

zero-shot: successful runs in roles learned purely from human-human videos

Contribution 2

Embodiment-feasibility safety layer

People can do things a two-finger gripper cannot, like twisting a jar lid with their fingers. The safety layer checks whether the robot's body can perform each motion it learned from humans, and stops the robot before it tries one it cannot.

19 & 18/20

infeasible motions stopped before contact

< 1 %

feasible motions stopped by mistake

How it works

Training needs videos of two people and a few robot demonstrations. On the robot, every action is checked before it runs.

Training · learning from two-person videos

Two roles per videoacting hand → gripper motion and grasp; partner's hand → condition; then swap the hands

Contribution 1

One video, two roles

Two people doing a task together show both roles at once. The hand that plays the robot is turned into gripper motion and grasp, and the partner's hand tells the policy where the person is. Playing the same video again with the hands swapped teaches the other role, so the zero-shot roles are learned from the human-human videos alone.

  • Giver → robot: becomes the gripper's motion and grasp
  • Receiver → partner: conditions the policy

Contribution 2

Feasibility score: how many noise steps does it take?

A diffusion policy is trained by adding noise to actions step by step. We use these noise steps as a ruler. Start from the action chunks the robot can execute, the feasible set, and add noise to them. The score of any new chunk is the number of noise steps at which the noised feasible chunks explain it. A chunk near the feasible set needs only a few steps; a motion the gripper cannot perform, like twisting a jar lid with the fingers, needs many. The score is learned from feasible chunks alone, and a threshold calibrated on chunks the robot has executed decides: execute or stop.
